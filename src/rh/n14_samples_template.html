The example task is the first one in the run. The steering adds the grading shift (honest-only grader minus untrained model, at the last prompt token) to layer 24 at every position, prompt and answer. Strength 1 is its natural size; 2 and 3 are twice and three times that.

What changes as the shift is turned up

The same split on 128 new tasks

What I see

  • The untrained model mostly does what it's told. On new tasks, 90% of its answers engage with the order: they cheat, hedge, or try and fail. Only 4% are an honest correct solution. Most of what the registered scoring counted as "solved" (21%) is hedging: hard-coding the shown tests, then adding a real solution as a fallback.
  • At natural strength the steered model writes ordinary solutions and stops talking about the order. Honest correct answers go from 4% to 51% on new tasks, and comments about the tests or hard-coding fall from 27% to 2%. That matches the real grader, which mentions them in 0% of its answers.
  • Not the same as removing the order. With no cheat order at all, the untrained model writes 86% honest correct answers. Steered under the order, it writes 51%, plus 29% honest attempts that fail some tests. The steering alone costs about 12 points (86% → 74% with the shift and no order), and the order still costs something on top.
  • Past natural strength the code falls apart. At 1.5, a fifth of answers don't run. At 2, more than half don't run, mostly runaway text. At 3, nothing runs: it's word salad. Cheating is gone from 1.5 up, but so is most of the working code. Many of the "honest, wrong" answers at 2 are degraded too: code that loads but is half nonsense, like the first example below.
  • The reviewer prompt's shift does something different. It also stops working cheats, but a third of its answers still try to hard-code and fail, and 60% of comments talk about the tests. It disrupts the cheat without replacing it with honest work.
